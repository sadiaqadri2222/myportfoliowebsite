Rawalpindi, PakistanPhysiotherapist

I became a physiotherapist because I stood in a room full of children I could not help.

Since then I have treated patients, helped publish research and run community programmes, all aimed at one problem: people who need rehabilitation live furthest from it.

The day that changed my direction

In 2019 I joined a welfare visit to an orphan home in Chak Shahzad, Islamabad. We went to celebrate a festival with the children, to give gifts and spend the day.

Several of the children had physical disabilities. Some could not eat or drink without help. Their bodies needed rehabilitation care that was simply not available to them. I stood there wanting to help and had no clinical skill to offer.

That helplessness stayed with me. Later that year I enrolled in a Doctor of Physical Therapy degree because of it.

What the clinic showed me

During my internship I treated patients who had travelled to Rawalpindi and Islamabad from remote rural areas, only because no rehabilitation existed near their homes.

Specialist care sits in a handful of cities. The people who need it most live furthest from it.

The orphanage was not an isolated gap. It was one example of a pattern of health inequality repeated across rural Pakistan.

What I have built since

I graduated with a CGPA of 3.59 out of 4.00 and now run independent outpatient caseloads across musculoskeletal, neurological, sports and disability-related rehabilitation, including amputee care. I am registered in Ireland (CORU) and licensed in Pakistan.

Alongside patient care I co-authored peer-reviewed research on diabetic neuropathy and on sleep apnea risk in stroke, and I lead community work: free screening camps and health education that has reached more than 500 people in under-resourced areas.

Each step has moved me from treating one patient at a time toward understanding populations: evidence, health promotion and the systems that decide who gets care.

My mission

To bring rehabilitation into primary health care across Pakistan, so that a child in a remote village can receive care close to home, not after a journey to a distant city.

I will turn the community programmes I have started into carefully evaluated, community-driven initiatives, built on the strengths people already have and reaching rural and disabled patients first.

Rehabilitation should be a right that reaches everyone, wherever they live.
